Literature review · working draft

Visualizing voice and sound: designs, users, effects

Which visual displays of sound have been designed, static or real-time; in which settings and for whom (reading speech, training one's own voice, sound access for deaf and hard-of-hearing people); and what user studies found. The review is meant to locate the gap for our own direction: abstract displays built by rule from acoustic features and made readable through crossmodal correspondences.

  1. RQ1 What visual displays of sound have been designed? processing level × visual encoding
  2. RQ2 For which users and in which settings? hearing / DHH × external sound / own voice
  3. RQ3 How were they evaluated, and with what outcomes? study design; what was tested: discrimination, interpretation, learning, preference, experience
  4. RQ4 What accounts of perception inform these designs, and how do the authors explain why their displays work or fail? theory cited, design rationale, post-hoc explanation
Inclusion criteria

A study is in if it meets all four criteria

  1. 1Display

    A visual display computed from the acoustic signal, static or real-time. Exception: displays driven by MIDI, when designed for DHH users.

    Music notation and lyrics. Physiological biofeedback (ultrasound, electropalatography, EMG, electroglottography, laryngeal imaging), except as a comparison arm. Why

  2. 2Sound

    Human voice (speech, singing, nonverbal vocalizations) for any users. Environmental sound and music only when the display was designed for or tested with DHH users.

    Environmental sound or music shown to hearing users only.

  3. 3Users

    The display is meant to be used without specialist knowledge of sound analysis. Users can be hearing or DHH, any age, clinical or not. Training with the display within the study is allowed at any level and coded (levels).

    Studies whose only participants are specialists trained to analyse sound through visual representations (acousticians, phoneticians, audio engineers, bioacousticians). Such groups appear only as comparisons.

  4. 4Display evaluated in a user study

    Participants used the display and the study measured an outcome of it: whether it can be read, whether it is useful, its effect on learning or experience, or how it compares with other displays.

    System descriptions, demos and design rationales alone; studies where the display is only a tool and is not itself evaluated; music visualizations made as artistic or creative work without a user study.

Three strands

Included studies are grouped by setting. The strands organize the map; they are not inclusion criteria, and a study can sit in more than one.

Strand 1

Reading speech from displays

Viewers identify, match or discriminate speech sounds from a display of someone else's voice.

 Strand 2

Displays as voice-training feedback

People see a display of their own voice while learning to speak or sing; includes speech therapy and singing pedagogy.

 Strand 3

Sound visualization for deaf and hard-of-hearing users

Displays designed for or tested with DHH users.

RQ1 · Display types

What is computed from the sound, and how it is drawn

Rows: how far the display is from the signal. Columns: how it is drawn. A plot has axes; an abstract mapping turns a quantity into a visual property such as size, colour or shape; a symbol stands for a category. Pictures are demo renderings of the speech sample or schematics. Click a type to see its studies.
