Literature review · working draft

Visualizing voice and sound: designs, users, effects

Which visual displays of sound have been designed, static or real-time; in which settings and for whom (reading speech, training one's own voice, sound access for deaf and hard-of-hearing people); and what user studies found. The review is meant to locate the gap for our own direction: abstract displays built by rule from acoustic features and made readable through crossmodal correspondences.

  1. RQ1 What visual displays of sound have been designed? processing level × visual encoding
  2. RQ2 For which users and in which settings? hearing / DHH × external sound / own voice
  3. RQ3 How were they evaluated, and with what outcomes? study design; what was tested: discrimination, interpretation, learning, preference, experience
  4. RQ4 What accounts of perception inform these designs, and how do the authors explain why their displays work or fail? theory cited, design rationale, post-hoc explanation
Inclusion criteria

A study is in if it meets one of three criteria

Criterion 1

Reading speech from spectrographic displays

Viewers identify, match or discriminate speech sounds from a spectrogram or spectrogram-like picture.

 Criterion 2

Spectral displays as voice-training feedback

People see a display of their own voice while learning to speak or sing; includes speech therapy and singing pedagogy.

 Criterion 3

Sound visualization for deaf and hard-of-hearing users

Displays designed for or tested with DHH users. Not limited to spectrograms.

Applies to all three

  • The display represents sound and is computed from the acoustic signal, live or afterwards. One exception, criterion 3 only: displays driven by MIDI are included when they were designed for DHH users.
  • Viewers are non-experts. Perceptual training and familiarization are fine; taught acoustic phonetics is not (training level ≤ 2, see Method).
  • There is a user study: people used the display and something was measured (accuracy, learning, preference or experience).
  • Music visualizations made as artistic or creative work without a user study.
  • Music notation and lyrics: writing systems, not displays of the sound.
  • Expert spectrogram readers (kept as a comparison point only).
  • Physiological biofeedback that shows the vocal tract or muscles instead of the sound (ultrasound, electropalatography, EMG, electroglottography, laryngeal imaging), except as a comparison arm. Why

RQ1 · Display types

What is computed from the sound, and how it is drawn

Rows: how far the display is from the signal. Columns: how it is drawn. A plot has axes; an abstract mapping turns a quantity into a visual property such as size, colour or shape; a symbol stands for a category.
