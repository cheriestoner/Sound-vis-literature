Method

How studies were chosen and coded

Planned as a scoping review (PRISMA-ScR): a reproducible search, two-stage screening and a fixed charting form, with descriptive counts and narrative synthesis rather than meta-analysis. Until the structured search is run, the studies here come from purposive searching, so the gaps listed are gaps in what we have found, not proven gaps.

  1. RQ1 What visual displays of sound have been designed? processing level × visual encoding · columns displays, and processing, encoding in displays.csv
  2. RQ2 For which users and in which settings? hearing / DHH × external sound / own voice · target_users, target_task, whose_sound, dhh, sound_type
  3. RQ3 How were they evaluated, and with what outcomes? study design; what was tested · design, n, compared, outcome, untrained_interpretation, measures, finding
  4. RQ4 What accounts of perception inform these designs, and how do the authors explain why their displays work or fail? cited_theory, mapping_rationale, design_rationale, post_hoc_explanation

Inclusion criteria

A study is included if it meets all four criteria:

  1. Display. A visual display computed from the acoustic signal, static or real-time. The sound drives the display: the picture is produced from the sound by a fixed process, not by a person's interpretation of that particular sound (human involvement level 1–3 below). Exception: displays driven by symbolic music data such as MIDI are included when they were designed for DHH users; the displays column records the source, so these studies stay distinguishable from signal-based ones. Excluded: music notation and lyrics, which are writing systems rather than displays of the sound; physiological biofeedback (below).
  2. Sound. Human voice (speech, singing, nonverbal vocalizations) is included for all users. Environmental sound and music are included only when the display was designed for or tested with DHH users.
  3. Users. The display is meant to be used without specialist knowledge of sound analysis. Users can be hearing or DHH, any age, clinical or non-clinical. Training with the display within the study is allowed at any level and coded (below). Excluded: studies whose only participants are specialists trained to analyse sound through visual representations (acousticians, phoneticians, audio engineers, bioacousticians); such groups appear only as comparisons.
  4. Display evaluated in a user study. Participants used the display and the study measured an outcome of it: whether it can be read, whether it is useful, its effect on learning or experience, or how it compares with other displays. Excluded: a system description, a demo or a design rationale alone; studies where the display is only a tool and is not itself evaluated; music visualizations made as artistic or creative work without a user study.

Strands. Included studies are grouped into three strands by setting: (1) reading speech from displays of others' voices; (2) displays as feedback on one's own voice in speech or singing training, including speech therapy and singing pedagogy; (3) sound visualization designed for or tested with DHH users. The strands organize the map and are recorded in the criteria column; they are not inclusion criteria, and a study can sit in more than one.

Revised 1 Nov 2026: the rule “computed from the acoustic signal” previously excluded all MIDI-driven displays, including Nanayakkara et al. (2013). The user-study requirement now does the work of keeping out artistic music visualizers.

Added 9 Nov 2026: the “sound drives the display” rule and the human involvement levels, following discussion with David. Wizard-of-Oz studies are included and coded by the intended system.

Revised 9 Nov 2026 (criterion 3): the line is drawn by the knowledge a display requires, not by who the users are. “Experts” was defined by spectrogram reading and no longer fits displays that are not spectrograms; training is now a property of how the system is learned, not an exclusion rule.

Restructured 9 Nov 2026: the three criteria (reading spectrograms, spectral feedback, DHH) became criteria that every study must meet (five, then four after merging “display is the object of study” with “user study”), and the three settings became strands. Under the new form, criteria 1–2 are no longer limited to spectral displays, and training given within a study no longer excludes it.

Physiological biofeedback is excluded: displays of the articulators or muscles rather than of the sound, such as ultrasound tongue imaging, electropalatography, electromagnetic articulography, surface EMG, electroglottography and laryngeal endoscopy. Reasons: (1) they show how a sound is made, not what it sounds like, while this review is about visual representations of the acoustic signal; (2) they need sensors on or in the speaker, so they cannot show other people's voices or everyday sounds and do not apply to reading others' sound or to DHH sound access. They appear only where a study compares them with an acoustic display (e.g. McAllister et al., 2025).

Human involvement

The display must be caused by the sound: if the input sound changes, the picture changes with it, through a process that does not depend on a person's reading of that sound. Every mapping embeds human choices (pitch upwards, anger in red), so the question is not whether people made choices but when. Choices made at design time, before any particular sound arrives, are fixed into the process and apply to every input. Choices made after hearing a particular sound replace the sound as the cause of the picture.

Test: given a new recording and no person present, does the system still produce the display?

LevelWhat the person doesIncluded
1 · Fully automaticSets the rules or trains the model in advance; the display then runs with no one in the loop. Models trained on human-labelled data count here when they run automatically.Yes
2 · Per-input tuningSets parameters for a specific input (e.g. a colour scheme for one song), after which the display is generated automatically. Variation within the input is driven by the sound; the overall setting is chosen by a person.Yes
3 · Human in the loopAdjusts the display while it runs (e.g. a live VJ) or selects among generated outputs.Yes
4 · Human interpretationListens and decides what the picture shows: drawing or painting to the sound, writing a prompt for an image generator, labelling the sound by hand and then mapping the labels.No

Generative models follow the same rule. Audio-conditioned generation (the sound or its features are the model's input) is level 1; a person who listens and writes the prompt is level 4. Random variation in the output does not break the link, because the sound still changes what can be generated, and a fixed seed makes the output reproducible.

Wizard-of-Oz studies are included. When researchers prepare or operate the display by hand to simulate a system meant to run automatically, the hand work is a stand-in for a process still to be built, not an interpretation that is part of the design. These studies are coded at the level of the intended system and flagged, since what participants saw may be cleaner or better timed than an automatic system would produce.

Recorded for each study as human_involvement (1–4, for the system as designed) and wizard_of_oz (yes · no), so the degree of automation can be described across the field. Not yet added to studies.csv.

Prior knowledge and training (criterion 3)

Prior knowledge. Naumann et al. (2007, p. 129) call a system intuitively usable when a user “is able to interact effectively, not-consciously using previous knowledge”, and order that knowledge on a continuum from innate and sensorimotor knowledge, through culture, to expertise (pp. 130–132; see also Blackler & Hurtienne, 2007). Hurtienne & Israel (2007) proposed image schemas as patterns for intuitive interaction. [Ours: criterion 3 excludes only the expertise end of this continuum, that is, displays read by applying specialist knowledge of sound analysis, such as acoustic-phonetic rules for spectrograms. Mappings grounded in crossmodal correspondences would draw on the innate or sensorimotor end.]

Training with the system. As in research on sensory substitution, training means learning to use the system within the study. It is allowed at any level and coded, because how much learning a display needs is one of the things the review compares. The line between levels 2 and 3 is whether participants work out the mapping from examples or are told the rules.

LevelTraining with the display
0 · NoneParticipants use the display without explanation
1 · OrientationBrief explanation of the mapping, or a few examples
2 · PracticeRepeated practice with feedback or labelled examples, without being told the mapping rules (perceptual learning)
3 · Explicit instructionSystematic teaching of the mapping rules (e.g. acoustic-phonetic rules for spectrograms)

Evidence of intuitiveness. Whether a display is intuitive is a result, not an inclusion rule. Stiles & Shimojo (2015) called a sensory-substitution mapping intuitive because naive participants, told nothing about the encoding and given no feedback, matched its sounds to images above chance, and they attributed this to crossmodal correspondences. Following them, intuitiveness is read from performance at training level 0, whatever the task. untrained_interpretation is a stricter test: saying what a sound is, not matching. Authors' own claims are coded separately (claims_intuitive), so claims and evidence can be compared.

What each source supports, and whether it has been checked, is listed under References. Text in [Ours: …] is our own reasoning.

The plan for a structured search (likely a scoping review following PRISMA-ScR), the decisions it needs and the areas not yet searched are in SEARCH.md. How the studies were evaluated

Included and pending studies by user-study design and strand. A study in two strands is counted in both.

Design types

  • Randomized controlled trial: participants randomly assigned to treatments.
  • Experiment: conditions or groups compared, or accuracy measured under controlled conditions.
  • Single-case design: a few participants, each measured repeatedly across phases or alternating treatments.
  • Case study: one participant, without a formal single-case design.
  • Single group: one group measured over time, no comparison condition.
  • Crowdsourcing: judgments collected from online volunteers.
  • Mixed methods: measures combined with interviews or design feedback.
  • Qualitative: interviews, observation or action research only.

Display matrix (RQ1)

Each display type in displays.csv is coded on two dimensions.

Processing level (processing): what is computed between the signal and the picture. Signal: the whole signal, transformed but not selected (waveform, spectrogram, spectrum). Feature: selected measurable quantities (F0, loudness, location). Inferred: the output of a recognizer (sound class, emotion). Learned: a neural network's latent space. Symbolic: note data such as MIDI, not audio. Physiological: not sound at all; outside scope.

Visual encoding (encoding): how it is drawn. Plot: axes, read like a graph. Abstract mapping: a continuous quantity drives a visual property such as size, colour, shape, motion or typography, so a small change in the sound gives a small change in the picture. Symbol: a sign that stands for a category (icon, emoji, word); the picture stays the same until the category changes. Mixed displays are coded by their main encoding; expressive captions, for example, are text whose typography is an abstract mapping of prosody.

Theoretical lens (our analysis)

Five perspectives on cross-sensory perception, each turned into questions we answer for every display. These are our judgements of the display, kept separate from the theory the authors cite (cited_theory, RQ4): a pitch contour uses the pitch–height correspondence whether or not its authors mention it. Each lens is used on its own; together they do not form one theory. The lens_* columns need operational definitions checked on a pilot set and, ideally, a second coder.

1. Crossmodal correspondences

Consistent associations between features of different senses, such as matching high-pitched sounds with small, bright objects located high up (Spence, 2011).

  • lens_correspondence (free text): which correspondences the mapping uses, e.g. "pitch–height; loudness–size"; "none" if the mapping is arbitrary or learned.
  • lens_scale: perceptual · physical · mixed · not-applicable. Whether frequency and intensity are drawn on perceptual scales (log or mel frequency, loudness) or physical ones (linear Hz, dB). Hermes (1998) relates the effectiveness of visual intonation feedback to displays that show the features that matter for hearing.

2. Amodal properties

Properties that can be picked up by more than one sense, such as synchrony, rhythm, tempo and intensity change (Bahrick & Lickliter, 2000, following Gibson, 1969).

  • lens_time: in-time (the picture changes as the sound happens) · time-as-space (time is drawn on an axis, as in a spectrogram) · no-time (a snapshot or a label).
  • lens_amodal (several, ;): synchrony · rhythm · tempo · intensity · none. Which of them a viewer can pick up. When time is drawn as space, rhythm becomes spacing that has to be read, not perceived as rhythm.

3. Sensorimotor contingencies

Perceiving is mastering how sensory input changes with one's own actions (O'Regan & Noë, 2001).

  • lens_loop: closed (the viewer's own voice drives the display in real time) · exploratory (the viewer acts on the display to explore other sounds, e.g. by moving, selecting or replaying) · open (the viewer only watches).

4. Form and function in vocal signals

The acoustic form of vocalizations reflects their function: body size, arousal, distress, intent (Pisanski et al., 2022; Morton's motivation-structural rules, 1977; Ohala's frequency code, 1984).

  • lens_level (several, ;): acoustic (physical quantities) · perceptual (how it sounds) · functional (what the sound signals about the source).
  • lens_function_route: label (a recognizer names the function, e.g. an emotion emoji) · form (a visual form that carries the same function, e.g. a larger shape for a larger body) · not-applicable.

5. Auditory scene analysis

Hearing groups sound into objects and streams, such as one talker or one event (Bregman, 1990). Gabrys (1990) found cues spread across two visual objects harder to learn than cues within one.

  • lens_objects: yes · partly · no. Whether one auditory object appears as one visual object. In a spectrogram, the harmonics of one voice are separate lines: no.

Outcome types

Adapted from the evaluation scenarios of Lam, Bertini, Isenberg, Plaisant & Carpendale (2012), Empirical studies in information visualization: Seven scenarios, IEEE TVCG 18(9). Their “user performance”, “user experience” and “environments and work practices” map onto performance, preference/experience and practice. “Learning” is added because voice-training studies measure change in the user's own production over time, which their scenarios do not cover.

  • Performance: accuracy or speed of a task done with the display (identify, match, detect).
  • Learning: change over training, including transfer to new items and retention without the display.
  • Preference: ratings or rankings of displays.
  • Experience: subjective states such as enjoyment, flow or self-efficacy.
  • Practice: interviews or observation of how the display is or would be used.

Outcome codes are ours, assigned from the measures listed in each paper; they are part of what needs checking (see TODO.md).

Open decisions

Studies affected by these are coded pending decision.

Do pitch displays count? Settled 9 Nov: yes

Most singing-feedback studies show a pitch contour, which is computed from the signal but is not spectral. Under the five criteria any display computed from the acoustic signal is in (criterion 1), so pitch displays are included. The affected studies are still coded pending until they are re-screened.

Environmental sound and music? Settled 9 Nov: DHH only

Most DHH sound-awareness displays target environmental sounds (doorbells, alarms, speech nearby), not voice. Criterion 2 includes environmental sound and music only when the display was designed for or tested with DHH users, because those studies test the same question for the same users: what a computed picture of sound conveys without hearing.

Use Zhang (2026) with caution

Most of the 36 studies it reviews use pitch displays rather than spectral ones; evidence on spectral or formant feedback is thin. At least two references are misattributed: Lã & Fiuza (2022) appears as Bernardini et al. (2022), and Blanco, Tassani & Ramirez (2021) as García-García & Tejada (2021). Check every study it cites against the original before using it.

Not found so far

Possible gaps. A systematic search is needed before claiming any of them.

  1. Whether untrained viewers can read paralinguistic information (emotion, arousal, speaker sex, distress) from raw spectrograms, with clear ground truth.
  2. Eye-tracking studies of how people look at spectrograms.
  3. Systematic manipulation of rendering parameters (frequency scale, window length, dynamic range, colour map) and their effect on readability. The closest is House et al. (1966), on display size and aspect ratio.
