Method

How studies were chosen and coded

Planned as a scoping review (PRISMA-ScR): a reproducible search, two-stage screening and a fixed charting form, with descriptive counts and narrative synthesis rather than meta-analysis. Until the structured search is run, the studies here come from purposive searching, so the gaps listed are gaps in what we have found, not proven gaps.

  1. RQ1 What visual displays of sound have been designed? processing level × visual encoding · columns displays, and processing, encoding in displays.csv
  2. RQ2 For which users and in which settings? hearing / DHH × external sound / own voice · target_users, target_task, whose_sound, dhh, sound_type
  3. RQ3 How were they evaluated, and with what outcomes? study design; what was tested · design, n, compared, outcome, untrained_interpretation, measures, finding
  4. RQ4 What accounts of perception inform these designs, and how do the authors explain why their displays work or fail? cited_theory, mapping_rationale, design_rationale, post_hoc_explanation

Inclusion criteria

A study is included if it meets at least one criterion:

  1. Reading speech from spectrographic displays. Viewers identify, match or discriminate speech from a spectrogram or a spectrogram-like picture.
  2. Spectral or spectrographic displays as voice-training feedback. People see a display of their own voice while learning to speak or sing, including speech therapy and singing pedagogy. “Spectral” covers time–frequency displays (spectrograms) and frequency-only displays (spectra, e.g. LPC curves).
  3. Sound visualization for deaf and hard-of-hearing users. Displays designed for or tested with DHH users, not limited to spectrograms.

and all of the following hold:

  • The display is computed from the acoustic signal, live or afterwards. Exception (criterion 3 only): displays driven by symbolic music data such as MIDI are included when they were designed for DHH users. The displays column records the source, so these studies stay distinguishable from signal-based ones.
  • Viewers are non-experts: training level 0–2 below. Expert readers appear only as a comparison point.
  • There is a user study: people used the display and something was measured (accuracy, learning, preference or experience). A system description, a demo or a design rationale alone is not enough.

Excluded: music visualizations made as artistic or creative work without a user study; music notation and lyrics, which are writing systems rather than displays of the sound.

Revised 1 Nov 2026: the rule “computed from the acoustic signal” previously excluded all MIDI-driven displays, including Nanayakkara et al. (2013). The user-study requirement now does the work of keeping out artistic music visualizers.

Physiological biofeedback is excluded: displays of the articulators or muscles rather than of the sound, such as ultrasound tongue imaging, electropalatography, electromagnetic articulography, surface EMG, electroglottography and laryngeal endoscopy. Reasons: (1) they show how a sound is made, not what it sounds like, while this review is about visual representations of the acoustic signal; (2) they need sensors on or in the speaker, so they cannot show other people's voices or everyday sounds and do not apply to reading others' sound or to DHH sound access. They appear only where a study compares them with an acoustic display (e.g. McAllister et al., 2025).

Training level (criterion 1)

Inclusion depends on what was trained, not how long. The line between levels 2 and 3 is whether participants work out the mapping from examples or are told the rules. Criterion 2 is exempt: in voice training, being told what the target is belongs to the method.

Review type and search

So far a conceptual review: studies were found by purposive searching, reference lists and snowballing, not by a documented database search. The plan for a structured search (likely a scoping review following PRISMA-ScR), the decisions it needs and the areas not yet searched are in SEARCH.md. The found_via column records how each study was found; all current ones are prior.

How the studies were evaluated

Included and pending studies by user-study design and criterion. A study coded for two criteria is counted in both.

Design types

  • Randomized controlled trial: participants randomly assigned to treatments.
  • Experiment: conditions or groups compared, or accuracy measured under controlled conditions.
  • Single-case design: a few participants, each measured repeatedly across phases or alternating treatments.
  • Case study: one participant, without a formal single-case design.
  • Single group: one group measured over time, no comparison condition.
  • Crowdsourcing: judgments collected from online volunteers.
  • Mixed methods: measures combined with interviews or design feedback.
  • Qualitative: interviews, observation or action research only.

Display matrix (RQ1)

Each display type in displays.csv is coded on two dimensions.

Processing level (processing): what is computed between the signal and the picture. Signal: the whole signal, transformed but not selected (waveform, spectrogram, spectrum). Feature: selected measurable quantities (F0, loudness, location). Inferred: the output of a recognizer (sound class, emotion). Learned: a neural network's latent space. Symbolic: note data such as MIDI, not audio. Physiological: not sound at all; outside scope.

Visual encoding (encoding): how it is drawn. Plot: axes, read like a graph. Abstract mapping: a continuous quantity drives a visual property such as size, colour, shape, motion or typography, so a small change in the sound gives a small change in the picture. Symbol: a sign that stands for a category (icon, emoji, word); the picture stays the same until the category changes. Mixed displays are coded by their main encoding; expressive captions, for example, are text whose typography is an abstract mapping of prosody.

Theoretical lens (our analysis)

Five perspectives on cross-sensory perception, each turned into questions we answer for every display. These are our judgements of the display, kept separate from the theory the authors cite (cited_theory, RQ4): a pitch contour uses the pitch–height correspondence whether or not its authors mention it. Each lens is used on its own; together they do not form one theory. The lens_* columns need operational definitions checked on a pilot set and, ideally, a second coder.

1. Crossmodal correspondences

Consistent associations between features of different senses, such as pitch with elevation, size or brightness, and loudness with size (Spence, 2011†).

  • lens_correspondence (free text): which correspondences the mapping uses, e.g. "pitch–height; loudness–size"; "none" if the mapping is arbitrary or learned.
  • lens_scale: perceptual · physical · mixed · not-applicable. Whether frequency and intensity are drawn on perceptual scales (log or mel frequency, loudness) or physical ones (linear Hz, dB). Hermes (1998)† argues a pitch display should keep only what is audible.

2. Amodal properties

Properties that can be picked up by more than one sense, such as synchrony, rhythm, tempo and intensity change (Gibson's amodal invariants; Bahrick & Lickliter's intersensory redundancy†).

  • lens_time: in-time (the picture changes as the sound happens) · time-as-space (time is drawn on an axis, as in a spectrogram) · no-time (a snapshot or a label).
  • lens_amodal (several, ;): synchrony · rhythm · tempo · intensity · none. Which of them a viewer can pick up. When time is drawn as space, rhythm becomes spacing that has to be read, not perceived as rhythm.

3. Sensorimotor contingencies

Perceiving is mastering how sensory input changes with one's own actions (O'Regan & Noë, 2001†).

  • lens_loop: closed (the viewer's own voice drives the display in real time) · exploratory (the viewer acts on the display to explore other sounds, e.g. by moving, selecting or replaying) · open (the viewer only watches).

4. Form and function in vocal signals

The acoustic form of vocalizations reflects their function: body size, arousal, distress, intent (Pisanski et al., 2022; Morton's motivation-structural rules, 1977†; Ohala's frequency code, 1984†).

  • lens_level (several, ;): acoustic (physical quantities) · perceptual (how it sounds) · functional (what the sound signals about the source).
  • lens_function_route: label (a recognizer names the function, e.g. an emotion emoji) · form (a visual form that carries the same function, e.g. a larger shape for a larger body) · not-applicable.

5. Auditory scene analysis

Hearing groups sound into objects and streams, such as one talker or one event (Bregman, 1990†). Gabrys (1990) found cues spread across two visual objects harder to learn than cues within one.

  • lens_objects: yes · partly · no. Whether one auditory object appears as one visual object. In a spectrogram, the harmonics of one voice are separate lines: no.

† From memory; check before citing (listed in TODO.md).

Outcome types

Adapted from the evaluation scenarios of Lam, Bertini, Isenberg, Plaisant & Carpendale (2012), Empirical studies in information visualization: Seven scenarios, IEEE TVCG 18(9). Their “user performance”, “user experience” and “environments and work practices” map onto performance, preference/experience and practice. “Learning” is added because voice-training studies measure change in the user's own production over time, which their scenarios do not cover.

  • Performance: accuracy or speed of a task done with the display (identify, match, detect).
  • Learning: change over training, including transfer to new items and retention without the display.
  • Preference: ratings or rankings of displays.
  • Experience: subjective states such as enjoyment, flow or self-efficacy.
  • Practice: interviews or observation of how the display is or would be used.

Outcome codes are ours, assigned from the measures listed in each paper; they are part of what needs checking (see TODO.md).

Open decisions

Studies affected by these are coded pending decision.

Do pitch displays count under criterion 2?

Most singing-feedback studies show a pitch contour, which is computed from the signal but is not spectral. Zhang (2026) groups them with spectral feedback. Options: widen criterion 2 to “displays computed from the acoustic signal”, or keep it spectral and treat pitch studies as comparison.

Environmental sound under criterion 3?

Most DHH sound-awareness displays target environmental sounds (doorbells, alarms, speech nearby), not voice. They are currently included because they test the same question for the same users: what a computed picture of sound conveys without hearing. An alternative is to restrict criterion 3 to speech, vocalizations and environmental sound, excluding instrumental music.

Use Zhang (2026) with caution

Most of the 36 studies it reviews use pitch displays rather than spectral ones; evidence on spectral or formant feedback is thin. At least two references are misattributed: Lã & Fiuza (2022) appears as Bernardini et al. (2022), and Blanco, Tassani & Ramirez (2021) as García-García & Tejada (2021). Check every study it cites against the original before using it.

Not found so far

Possible gaps. A systematic search is needed before claiming any of them.

  1. Whether untrained viewers can read paralinguistic information (emotion, arousal, speaker sex, distress) from raw spectrograms, with clear ground truth.
  2. Eye-tracking studies of how people look at spectrograms.
  3. Systematic manipulation of rendering parameters (frequency scale, window length, dynamic range, colour map) and their effect on readability. The closest is House et al. (1966), on display size and aspect ratio.
